3. The script

Ricardo Ricardo (Business lead): hook (0:00–0:20)

"About two hours north of this room, people are hearing a sound all day and all night. Residents in Ashburn told NBC4 it's like a helicopter hovering over their neighborhood. News4 measured it at 90 decibels, a level they said calls for ear protection with long exposure. Tate has what people actually wrote."

Ricardo's delivery:

  • You set the energy for the whole pitch. Say the first sentence slowly, looking at the judges.
  • Pause one second after "all day and all night" and after "90 decibels."
  • Turn toward Tate as you say his name, and keep looking at him while he reads. The judges will look where you look.

Tate Tate: the evidence, in their own words (0:20–0:40)

(Tate reads from a printed card. Reading quotes from paper is normal and expected.)

"One resident posted: 'Anyone hear the low frequency hum in the area? I'm just over a mile from the nearest data center.'

Another wrote: 'I live 5 miles from there.'

And this is from Loudoun County's own website, about the way the county measures noise: '…measurements generally do not capture low frequency noise or the discomfort caused by steady tonal sounds.'"

Tate's delivery:

  • Hold the card at chest height, so your face stays visible.
  • Read slowly, one quote at a time. After each quote, look up at the judges for one second, then read the next one.
  • Stand still with your feet planted. When you finish, look back at Ricardo.
  • That's your whole part: three quotes, read slowly and clearly. Practice it ten times out loud.

Ricardo Ricardo: the problem (0:40–1:00)

"In plain English: the county's noise meter barely hears deep sounds, and this hum is almost all deep sound. And Loudoun's own page says some studies link low-frequency noise to effects ranging from discomfort to potential health complications. Virginia has more data centers than anywhere on Earth.

The law can't hear it. We figured out how to fix that."

Ricardo's delivery:

  • "In plain English" tells the judges you're about to make the quote simple. Say it, pause, then deliver the translation.
  • "The law can't hear it" is the line the whole pitch comes back to. Say it slower than anything else in your part. Then turn toward Vihaan.

Vihaan Vihaan (Technical lead): the science (1:00–1:50)

(Vihaan holds a phone in both hands at waist level. He uses it in gesture 3.)

TonalWatch measures twice: once the official way, and once with the low sounds left in. (Gesture 2.) If the second reading is much louder, that gap is the hum the law misses.

It's not a certified meter, and we say so. It doesn't replace the county's measurement. It gets one ordered. Anthony, show them."

(Only if true, add one line: "I've heard this hum from my own neighborhood." A personal line is the strongest credibility you have.)

Vihaan's three gestures (exactly these, nothing else):

Vihaan's delivery:

  • Your sentences are short on purpose. Say each one, then stop. Let your voice go down at the end of each sentence, so it sounds like a fact, not a question.
  • Memorize the bold sentences word for word. They are your whole argument.
  • Eyes: pick two judges. Look at one for a sentence, then the other. That's enough eye contact, and it looks natural.
  • Your last line, "It gets one ordered," is a strength, so say it with the most confidence of anything in your part.

4. Why these words work (so you deliver them on purpose)

  • We start close to home. "Two hours north of this room" makes the problem feel near, and near problems feel urgent.
  • Real people's words beat statistics. That's why Tate reads what residents actually wrote.
  • Every big number gets a meaning. "90 decibels" means nothing alone; "a level that calls for ear protection" does.
  • No jargon the judges have to decode. On stage we never say "dBA," "dBC" or "C minus A." Judges who have to decode stop listening. Use the words in the table below.
  • Every problem is followed by what we do about it. Fear with no solution makes people tune out.
  • Judges are told where to look before the moment happens. That's why Anthony says "Watch this one number" before the jump.
  • Parts end on strengths, not limits. Vihaan admits the limit in the middle and ends on "It gets one ordered."
  • The ending repeats the beginning. "The law can't hear it" opens the problem and closes the pitch, and the very last word is our product name.
  • We always say "we." Credit goes to the team, which is exactly what Team Chemistry scores.

5. Speaking techniques (everyone)

Voice

  • Slow down. Nerves make everyone talk fast. Aim for a calm pace, about 2–3 words per second.
  • Project to the judge farthest from you. If they can hear you easily, everyone can.
  • Stress the bold words. They carry the meaning; everything else can be lighter.
  • End sentences going down, not up. Going up sounds like a question; going down sounds certain.

Pauses instead of filler

  • When you feel "um," "like" or "so" coming, pause instead. A one-second pause sounds confident; a filler word sounds unsure.
  • Pause after every big number and after the demo shows the jump. Silence lets it land.

Eyes

  • One sentence, one judge. Look at one judge for a full sentence, then move to the next.
  • Don't look at the screen while speaking, except Anthony while pointing at something.

Body

  • Feet shoulder-width apart, weight even. No swaying or shifting.
  • Hands at waist level, out of your pockets. If you don't know what to do with them, hold your card or phone with both hands.
  • Smile when your part starts. It relaxes you and the judges.

While someone else is speaking

  • Look at the speaker (or at the screen during the demo) and nod naturally. Judges watch the whole team, not just the speaker.
  • No checking phones, no fidgeting, no whispering.

Handoffs (this is where Team Chemistry points come from)

  • Say the next person's name and turn toward them as you say it.
  • The next speaker starts within one second. Don't wait for a pause to fill.

If you blank

  • Take one breath and jump to your handoff line. Skipping a sentence is invisible; rambling is not.
  • Teammates never prompt out loud. If someone freezes, the next speaker starts their part.
  • Never apologize ("sorry, I'm nervous"). Just continue.

Memorizing

  • Memorize your first sentence and last sentence word for word. Know the middle by its ideas, so it sounds natural.
  • No reading from a phone screen. Tate's quote card is the only script on stage.

6. Judge questions (2 minutes, 10 points)

Judges will often point at one person and ask them directly. Everyone needs a short, confident answer ready, even if it's just the basic version.

The rules:

  1. Asked by name? You answer first. Nobody jumps in before you've given your basic answer.
  2. Asked to the whole team? The owner in the table answers.
  3. Technical questions (how it works, the math, the code, accuracy) go to Vihaan. If you're asked one, give the one-line basic answer below, then hand off: "Vihaan designed the analysis, so he can go deeper."
  4. Two sentences, then stop. Short answers leave time for more questions, which means more chances to score.
  5. Don't know? "Honest answer: today's version doesn't do that. Here's how we'd add it." Never guess a number or make up technical details.

Everyone memorizes this one-sentence answer for "So what does it do?":

"It checks whether a hum is the low-frequency machine noise the law can't measure, and turns it into a report a resident can attach to a complaint."

How to answer well:

  • Listen to the whole question. Don't start answering halfway through.
  • Pause one second before answering. It looks thoughtful, not slow.
  • Start by looking at the judge who asked, then include the other judges.
  • End firmly on your last word. No trailing "...so yeah."

Vihaan, for your answers: you know this deeply, so the risk is saying too much. Use this pattern: one clear headline sentence, one reason, then stop and offer: "Happy to go deeper on that." Let the judge ask for more.

7. What we can and can't say

This section uses the technical terms so you know exactly what's true. When you say any of it out loud, use the plain words from section 4.

Facts you can say (verified):

  • Loudoun's page: "dBA measurements generally do not capture low frequency noise or the discomfort caused by steady tonal sounds"; "some studies suggest low frequency sound may pose adverse impacts ranging from general discomfort to potential health complications"; no policy or regulatory language specific to tonal noise; amendments anticipated spring or summer 2027. loudoun.gov/6405/Noise-Air-Quality-Concerns
  • Prince William County's steady tonal noise rules took effect May 1, 2026, are measured in C-weighting at the complainant's property line, and define steady tonal noise as not fluctuating more than 3 dBC. pwcva.gov/department/public-works/steady-tonal-noise
  • NBC4 (March 23, 2026): residents compared it to a helicopter hovering all day and night; News4 measured 90 decibels using an app, "a level considered high and requires ear protection if there is sustained exposure"; residents up to five miles away reported hearing it; zoning enforcement said it would investigate. nbcwashington.com/news/local/northern-virginia/neighbors-raise-concerns-about-noisy-data-center-in-loudoun/4080249
  • DeFlock: started by one software engineer as a side project with no advertising campaign; volunteers have mapped more than 16,000 license-plate cameras; by August 2026 Flock cameras had become a midterm election issue. 404media.co/flock-threatens-open-source-developer-mapping-its-surveillance-cameras · denvergazette.com/2026/08/28/deflock-started-from-one-mans-curiosity-now-flock-cameras-are-a-midterm-issue
  • C-minus-A is a WHO-recommended low-frequency indicator; above 20 dB warrants further investigation (Leventhall). It is an indicator, not proof.

How to describe the app (say it exactly this way):

  • Two screening checks: the C-minus-A difference (15 dB prototype screen) and an experimental sustained-peak check (a narrow peak present across most of the 5 seconds), plus recording-quality flags.
  • Levels are uncalibrated digital levels, not real dBA/dBC sound-pressure readings.
  • It produces a printable observation report with the spectrum, notes and official sources. It does not file complaints. The map shows Loudoun County's data center buildings (county GIS) and demo readings; your own observations stay in your browser, so there is no shared public map yet.

Never say these (false or unverified):

  • "Nothing like this exists." A Data Center Noise app, dcmeasure.com and a UCCS monitoring lab exist. Say "every existing tool gives one number."
  • "Nothing flagged" for the 1 kHz tone. The peak check does find the tone; say "Not low-frequency."
  • "It runs the legal tests" / "three tests from the standards." The checks are screens, not standardized tonality or the county's legal definition.
  • "It files the complaint for you" / "our map of readings." It builds a report to attach; the map is "what's next."
  • "We coded it ourselves." AI agents wrote most of the code. Say what we decided and verified.
  • "It causes health problems." Say "some studies link it to effects from discomfort to potential health complications," and credit Loudoun's page.
  • "PWC uses the same A-vs-C gap." Say "PWC switched to C-weighting because A-weighting misses this."
  • "73 dBC day / 68 dBC night." Not confirmed in the ordinance text; don't quote specific limits.
  • "Proves a violation." Say "indicates a low-frequency hum worth a professional measurement."

8. How to practice your part

  • Read your part out loud five times. Reading silently doesn't train your mouth.
  • Time yourself. Your part should fit its slot with a few seconds to spare.
  • Record yourself on your phone once and listen back for filler words and speed.
  • Practice your handoff line with the next speaker, so the switch is instant.
  • Have a teammate ask you two random questions from section 6, and answer each in two sentences.
  • Run the full 5 minutes standing up, in speaking order, with the timer (button at the top of the page).

Extra practice by person:

  • Ricardo: you speak three times, so practice the transitions: hook into Tate, problem into Vihaan, market into Anthony. Practice the DeFlock story until it sounds like a story you're telling, not reading.
  • Tate: read your three quotes out loud ten times, slowly, looking up after each one. Memorize the one-sentence "what does it do" answer and the environmental-issue answer.
  • Vihaan: practice the three gestures in a mirror until "down" and "flat" look clearly different, then practice your part in front of one teammate with the two-judge eye pattern. For Q&A, use headline-reason-stop; have someone stop you at 20 seconds.
  • Anthony: run the full demo table on the presentation laptop at least five times, including the setup list. Practice the honest AI answer until it sounds confident, not defensive. Memorize the closing line word for word.
